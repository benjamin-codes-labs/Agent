## [S1] Backscattered electron contrast and mean atomic number
- url: TBD
- status: UNVERIFIED STUB
- topic: detector physics

The backscattered electron yield rises with the mean atomic number of the
material under the beam, so in a backscattered electron image a transition
metal oxide active material appears brighter than a carbon-rich binder domain,
and open pore space appears darkest of all. This ordering is what allows phase
fractions to be estimated from brightness in a cross-section, provided the
image has not been contrast-stretched per field of view.

Because the signal depends on composition rather than on surface shape, a
backscattered electron image is the preferred basis for measuring the active
material fraction, particle size and particle shape.

## [S2] Secondary electron detectors and topographic contrast
- url: TBD
- status: UNVERIFIED STUB
- topic: detector physics

A chamber-mounted Everhart-Thornley detector collects secondary electrons that
have scattered along a line of sight to the detector, which produces strong
topographic contrast and makes edges, relief and local charging prominent. An
in-column detector collects low-energy secondary electrons emitted close to the
surface and is therefore more sensitive to fine surface detail at the expense of
topographic shading.

In practice the in-column image is the better basis for judging binder coverage
and binder texture, while the chamber detector is the better basis for pore
edges and for cross-checking cracks.

## [S3] Electrode processing and the pore network
- url: TBD
- status: UNVERIFIED STUB
- topic: manufacturing

Calendering compresses a dried electrode coating to a target thickness, which
reduces total porosity and tends to flatten and align the remaining pore space.
Two electrodes with the same active material and the same formulation can
therefore present clearly different porosity and pore shape if they were
calendered to different densities.

Mixing conditions govern how evenly the conductive carbon and binder are
distributed. Poor dispersion leaves binder-rich agglomerates and regions of bare
active material surface, which shows up as a wider spread of local binder
coverage rather than as a change in the average.

## [S4] Particle cracking in cross-section
- url: TBD
- status: UNVERIFIED STUB
- topic: microstructure

Cracks within active material particles appear in cross-section as narrow dark
lines that start at a particle boundary or run between internal grain
boundaries, and they are distinguished from pores by their elongated shape and
near-constant width. Cracking is reported both as a consequence of calendering
pressure and as a consequence of repeated cycling, so crack density in a
cross-section of an unknown sample does not by itself identify which of the two
produced it.

## [S5] Cross-sectional particle size statistics
- url: TBD
- status: UNVERIFIED STUB
- topic: measurement caveats

A particle size distribution measured from a two-dimensional cross-section is
biased relative to the true three-dimensional distribution, because a random
section through a sphere rarely passes through its centre. Percentiles derived
from cross-sections are therefore useful for comparing samples prepared and
imaged the same way, and are not comparable with a laser diffraction
distribution supplied by a material vendor.
